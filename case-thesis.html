Finding fraud in 5.4 million municipal invoices

Four years of Gothenburg City's payments, read by a system I built from scratch. It pointed at one association. PwC's own investigation later confirmed what it found.

What this is

A fraud detector for public money

Every year Gothenburg City pays out billions of SEK across millions of invoices. No auditor can read them all, so irregularities can hide in plain sight for years. For my master's thesis I built a system that reads every single one and points to the suppliers that deserve a closer look.

The system narrowed 18,774 suppliers down to 31 high-risk candidates, and uncovered 13 control failures around a single association. Months later, PwC's own investigation reached the same conclusions.

How it was done

From 48 CSV files to a shortlist of 31

Collect. Four years of open payment data, 48 monthly CSV files with mixed encodings and text-formatted amounts, cleaned and combined into one dataset of 5,433,668 transactions. No rows excluded.

Design. A modular system with seven parts: three statistical cores and four support modules. Suppliers are profiled on transaction patterns and scored 0 to 100, with bonuses for dangerous combinations like uniform amounts from a supplier that suddenly disappears.

Iterate. Nine versions, each measured against the last. The breakthrough was the coefficient of variation: suppliers invoicing suspiciously uniform amounts stood out. Standardized care rates caused half of all false alarms and got their own adjustment. The shortlist went from 1,516 suppliers to 31, a 97 percent reduction.

Read between the lines. Statistics alone were not enough; every top candidate turned out to have a legitimate explanation. So the system also reads what invoices say. A sports club billing "administrative services" to the education department is grammatically normal and semantically absurd. That lens surfaced 16 associations worth 13.2 MSEK.

Verify in the real world. Fourteen freedom-of-information requests, 221 pages of contracts, attestation records and bankruptcy filings, and a meeting with the city's leasing company turned suspicion into evidence.

The finding

One association, 13 control failures

Bergsjön Sportklubb received 2.16 MSEK in municipal funding, breached its contract six times, kept no bookkeeping and went bankrupt with zero assets. The same person who handled attestation exceptions also sat in the approval chain. In total, 13 broken controls, each one independently confirmed by PwC's investigation, Report 59-2023.

The market-leading screening tool used by 240 municipalities had not flagged any of it. Neither had my statistical modules. Only the semantic analysis found it, which became the thesis's central result.

Key insight: statistical anomalies in municipal invoices correlate more with legitimate business patterns than with fraud. The powerful combination is semantics plus public records: read what the invoices say, then demand the paperwork.
